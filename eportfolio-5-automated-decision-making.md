# E-Portfolio 5: Automated Decision-Making in Government Systems

## Introduction

Automated Decision-Making (ADM) refers to the use of computer systems to make or assist with decisions, often through predefined rules or artificial intelligence.

Having worked in government services, I initially understood ADM mainly as a tool for processing claims and improving efficiency. Throughout this unit, I have developed a broader understanding of its ethical implications, including fairness, transparency, accountability, accessibility and human oversight.

The following six artefacts explore both the benefits and risks of government ADM in Australia and internationally.

---

## Artefact 1: Automation in Government Welfare Systems

### Artefact

[Commonwealth Ombudsman (2025) — Automation in the Targeted Compliance Framework](https://www.ombudsman.gov.au/__data/assets/pdf_file/0017/320750/Automation-in-the-Targeted-Compliance-Framework.pdf)

<img width="956" height="1027" alt="image" src="https://github.com/user-attachments/assets/32bf8bb8-97ac-4389-ac0a-39ab25aa180c" />

### Summary

The Commonwealth Ombudsman's (2025) investigation found that income-support payments were unlawfully cancelled under the Targeted Compliance Framework because automated processes had not been properly aligned with legislative requirements. This demonstrates how standardised government decision-making can produce harmful outcomes when systems, rules and oversight are not kept consistent with the law.

### Justification and Reflection

Having processed JobSeeker claims at Centrelink in 2024, this report is particularly relevant to my own experience. I encountered a woman escaping domestic violence whose family home was initially treated as an assessable asset. Despite resistance, I continued questioning the outcome until I established her eligibility for JobSeeker. Although this involved a different process, it reinforced my belief that automated decision-making requires meaningful human judgement, clear escalation procedures and accountability. This aligns with the ACS principle of prioritising the public interest.

## Artefact 2: Government Transparency and Accountability

### Artefact

[Office of the Australian Information Commissioner (2026) — Automated Decision-Making and Public Reporting](https://www.oaic.gov.au/freedom-of-information/information-commissioner-decisions-and-reports/foi-reports/Automated-decision-making-and-public-reporting-under-the-Freedom-of-Information-Act)

<img width="957" height="1029" alt="image" src="https://github.com/user-attachments/assets/54778552-9adb-4f10-90b7-22dfcb3c562f" />

### Summary

The Office of the Australian Information Commissioner (OAIC 2026) reviewed 23 Australian government agencies authorised to use ADM. Only four (17%) explicitly disclosed using ADM in decisions affecting the public, highlighting limitations in government transparency. The report recommends clearer public reporting about automated decision-making processes and the systems influencing government decisions.

### Justification and Reflection

My experience at Centrelink taught me that meaningful review and appeal mechanisms already exist within government services. Citizens also have a fundamental right to understand how decisions affecting them are reached. This is particularly important in human services, where people's livelihoods may depend on those decisions. Transparency does not necessarily require revealing sensitive technical information. Rather, it ensures accountability and respects individuals' rights, reflecting both Kantian principles and the ACS Code of Professional Conduct.

## Artefact 3: Algorithmic Discrimination and Fairness

### Artefact

[UK Department for Work and Pensions (2025) — Universal Credit Advances Model Fairness Assessment](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/fairness-assessment-including-statistical-analysis-of-the-universal-credit-advances-machine-learning-model-1-april-2024-to-31-march-2025/universal-credit-advances-model-fairness-assessment)

<img width="958" height="1030" alt="image" src="https://github.com/user-attachments/assets/92fe41f9-a13e-4fa0-8742-02c0ca23a9f9" />

### Summary

The UK Department for Work and Pensions (2025) examined a machine-learning system that flags potentially fraudulent Universal Credit advance requests for human review. Claimants aged 55–65 were 2.8 times more likely to be flagged than those aged 35–44, while non-UK nationals were flagged at 2.27 times the rate of UK nationals. The department acknowledged these disparities and proposed further analysis and model retraining.

### Justification and Reflection

The demographic disparities highlighted in this report raise interesting ethical questions. I don't necessarily believe higher referral rates automatically indicate discrimination, as fraudsters may exploit characteristics associated with vulnerable populations. Even so, discrepancies between referral rates and actual outcomes still warrant investigation. Vulnerable groups deserve additional safeguards, particularly when automated decisions could adversely affect their financial security. My position reflects Kantian principles of fairness while acknowledging the practical benefits of detecting fraudulent claims.

## Artefact 4: Human Oversight Versus Automation

### Artefact

[Australian Human Rights Commission (2026) — Commissioners Welcome Ombudsman Probe of Automated Aged Care Assessments](https://humanrights.gov.au/about-us/media-centre/media-releases/older-peoples-rights/commissioners-welcome-ombudsman-probe-of-automated-aged-care-assessments)

<img width="951" height="1025" alt="image" src="https://github.com/user-attachments/assets/480785e8-622c-47bf-ad56-ae3d20733f6b" />

### Summary

The Australian Human Rights Commission (2026) welcomed an Ombudsman investigation into the Integrated Assessment Tool used to help determine access to government-subsidised aged care. Concerns included reports that qualified human assessors could not override algorithmic outcomes, even where their professional judgement differed. The investigation highlights the importance of transparency, accountability and meaningful human oversight in essential care services.

### Justification and Reflection

I believe the authority to override automated decisions should depend on demonstrated competence rather than simply holding a particular position. Experience, measurable performance and understanding of the system should help determine who receives this responsibility. Equally, capable employees shouldn't be unnecessarily restricted from exercising professional judgement. My experience at Centrelink reinforced the importance of this distinction. A transparent, measurable and auditable framework could provide a middle ground between consistency, accountability and meaningful human oversight.

## Artefact 5: AI-Assisted Welfare Monitoring in South Korea

### Artefact

[OECD (2025) — Digital Government Review of Korea](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/digital-government-review-of-korea_9defc197-en/full-report/delivering-human-centred-and-proactive-public-services_41783522.html)

<img width="957" height="1030" alt="image" src="https://github.com/user-attachments/assets/90582a7b-51c8-4e1f-a818-84d66640ff2d" />

### Summary

The OECD's (2025) Digital Government Review of Korea describes South Korea's AI-assisted telephone outreach programme for vulnerable residents. The system contacts elderly and socially isolated individuals, analysing their responses to identify potential welfare needs or distress. When concerns arise, human welfare workers are alerted to provide assistance. The programme also incorporates informed consent and data protection safeguards.

### Justification and Reflection

This programme demonstrates an application of AI that I strongly support. Vulnerable people may lack the ability or opportunity to access government support independently, making proactive identification particularly valuable. Importantly, the technology acts as an identification aid rather than replacing social workers, who remain responsible for providing assistance. With appropriate consent and privacy safeguards, I believe this represents a sensible balance between technological efficiency and human involvement, consistent with both utilitarian benefits and Kantian respect for human dignity.

## Artefact 6: Regulation and Ethical Governance of AI

### Artefact

[Digital Transformation Agency (2025) — Policy for the Responsible Use of AI in Government, Version 2.0](https://www.digital.gov.au/ai/ai-in-government-policy)

<img width="958" height="1029" alt="image" src="https://github.com/user-attachments/assets/50d2f447-434b-4c99-a3a2-28ddd90df55e" />

### Summary

The Digital Transformation Agency's (2025) updated policy establishes requirements for responsible AI use across relevant Commonwealth government agencies. Effective from December 2025, it requires designated accountability, transparency statements, internal AI registers, staff training and impact assessments. These measures aim to strengthen oversight and identify ethical risks before and during deployment, rather than responding only after harm occurs.

### Justification and Reflection

Preventing foreseeable harm should take priority over addressing problems after they occur, particularly when automated government decisions affect vulnerable people. My experience working in government services demonstrated the importance of clear procedures and accountability, although compliance alone doesn't necessarily guarantee ethical outcomes. I believe ongoing evaluation is essential to ensure automated systems remain fair and effective. This reflects my fundamentally Kantian perspective, complemented by Rule Utilitarian consideration of consequences and the ACS principle of prioritising the public interest.
