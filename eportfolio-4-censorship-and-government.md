This e-portfolio presents four artefacts on censorship, regulation and society.

## Artefact 1: South Korea's Emergency Blocking of Copyright-Infringing Websites

### Artefact

Link: https://www.mcst.go.kr/english/policy/pressView.jsp?pSeq=629

Image:
<img width="952" height="1024" alt="image" src="https://github.com/user-attachments/assets/fad4a0e8-8d50-4a06-9a81-6abadeeb60a9" />

### Summary

In 2026, South Korea introduced emergency and access-blocking systems for copyright-infringing websites under its revised Copyright Act. Soon after implementation, the government reported that operators were already bypassing ISP blocks, prompting a review of the technical causes and possible improvements (Ministry of Culture, Sports and Tourism 2026a; 2026b).

### Justification and Reflection

I chose this artefact because it connects directly to the Week 9 discussion about whether websites should still be blocked when bypasses are possible (Galea 2026). I think blocking can reduce casual access, but for technically capable users it becomes more of a cat-and-mouse game. From speaking with Chinese people and spending time in China, I noticed VPN knowledge seemed more common, particularly among younger users, than in Australia. This reinforced my view that stronger censorship can encourage people to learn how to bypass it. ICT professionals therefore need to be realistic about what blocking can achieve.

## Artefact 2: UK Services Geo-Blocking Users

### Artefact

Link: https://www.ofcom.org.uk/online-safety/protecting-children/enforcement-bulletin-enforcement-programme-to-protect-children-from-harmful-content-through-the-use-of-age-assurance

Image:
<img width="958" height="1026" alt="image" src="https://github.com/user-attachments/assets/f13ac2c5-604c-4c88-a94e-5201b886ee75" />

### Summary

In August 2026, Ofcom reported that seven services hosting particularly harmful content had geo-blocked the UK after regulatory engagement. Rather than introduce the required age-assurance measures, the providers made their services unavailable to UK users (Ofcom 2026).

### Justification and Reflection

I chose this artefact because the result went beyond the regulation's original target. The providers made their own decision to withdraw UK access, but in hindsight a more targeted approach may have worked better. It also made me question whether enough investigation, modelling or testing had been done to anticipate this response. This connects to Week 9's discussion of national regulation on a global internet (Galea 2026). For ICT professionals, good governance means considering how organisations may actually respond to a control, not only what the control is intended to achieve.

## Artefact 3: EU Privacy-Preserving Age Verification

### Artefact

Link: https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/news/commission-urges-member-states-rollout-eu-age-verification-app

Image:
<img width="950" height="1025" alt="image" src="https://github.com/user-attachments/assets/4516575c-0b8e-44b9-a2cd-f19ec00ea035" />

### Summary

In April 2026, the European Commission urged member states to roll out a privacy-preserving age-verification app. It allows users to prove they meet an age requirement without revealing their exact age, identity or other personal details (European Commission 2026).

### Justification and Reflection

I chose this artefact because I think age restrictions can be reasonable for some online content, but implementation matters. Protecting children matters more to me than avoiding every inconvenience, but services should collect no more personal information than necessary. I would be comfortable with age estimation if the data were used only for verification and not retained. This relates to Week 9's discussion of classification and identifying adult and child users online (Galea 2026). It showed me how ICT professionals can design restrictions that are more targeted while reducing unnecessary privacy impacts.


## Artefact 4: Week 9 Workshop Reflection

### Artefact

Image:
<img width="1911" height="1027" alt="Screenshot 2026-09-15 105248" src="https://github.com/user-attachments/assets/721185f2-846d-47ac-a453-79f50644d6df" />

### Summary

The Week 9 workshop explored government censorship, internet blocking, classification and private-company responsibility. During the discussion, I commented that education can help but needs to be continuous rather than treated as a one-off solution. I also raised the concern that platforms can cause significant harm when they fail to protect users and profit from harmful content or behaviour (Galea 2026).

### Justification and Reflection

The workshop reinforced my view that education, personal responsibility and company responsibility all matter. Censorship alone cannot solve these problems, but companies should not knowingly benefit from foreseeable harm. If reducing that harm costs engagement or revenue, I think they should accept the loss. For me, the ICT-professional lesson is that ethical responsibility can go beyond legal compliance when systems we design or manage contribute to harmful outcomes.
