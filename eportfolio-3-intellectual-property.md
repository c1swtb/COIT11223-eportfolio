## Artefact 1: AI-Generated Music and Intellectual Property

### Artefact

**Link:** https://www.aria.com.au/charts/news/aria-charts-set-eligibility-rules-for-recordings-made-with-ai

<img width="1179" height="1029" alt="Screenshot 2026-09-09 185401" src="https://github.com/user-attachments/assets/7eec1ad2-9bf4-48d7-a2e0-d05e513b33e1" />

### Summary

ARIA updated its chart rules in August 2026 so wholly AI-generated recordings are ineligible, while recordings using generative AI in a supporting role can remain eligible if they are substantially human-made (Australian Recording Industry Association 2026).

### Justification and reflection

For the time being, I think a cautious approach makes sense. AI can assist production or the creative process, but fully generated music raises questions about authorship and ownership. The line may be clear in principle but difficult to apply, echoing the Week 7 discussion. I am most concerned when AI output closely reproduces existing work. I would not place primary responsibility on the AI company because it provides the tool. Responsibility should depend partly on whether the person releasing the work is transparent, while platforms also share responsibility when they commercialise it.

## Artefact 2: Nintendo v Pocketpair Patent Dispute

### Artefact

**Link:** https://www.reuters.com/technology/nintendo-pokemon-sue-palworld-producer-patent-infringement-2024-09-19/

<img width="668" height="984" alt="image" src="https://github.com/user-attachments/assets/4b8df947-7599-46ab-82f2-5cf214d01254" />

### Summary

Reuters (2024) reported that Nintendo and The Pokémon Company sued Pocketpair, developer of *Palworld*, for patent infringement, seeking an injunction and damages. Pocketpair later said the litigation led it to change gameplay involving Pal Spheres while it continued disputing the claims (Pocketpair 2025).

### Justification and reflection

I think Nintendo's action is an overreach. Protecting specific code or a genuinely novel technical implementation is reasonable, but broad gameplay ideas should only be patentable in extreme cases. Catching a creature using an object is not far removed from catching an animal with a net, so I struggle to see that concept as proprietary innovation. My greater concern is the imbalance of resources: a large company can pressure a smaller developer through litigation over broad ideas, potentially discouraging smaller developers from experimenting with familiar mechanics.

## Artefact 3: Australia's Orphan Works Copyright Scheme

### Artefact

**Link:** https://ministers.ag.gov.au/media-centre/important-reforms-australias-copyright-framework-pass-parliament-01-04-2026

<img width="941" height="979" alt="Screenshot 2026-09-09 194631" src="https://github.com/user-attachments/assets/71733fcb-3048-448a-aec8-0dc513d60761" />

### Summary

In April 2026, Australia passed its first orphan works scheme for copyright material whose owner is unknown or cannot be located. The reforms expand access to creative, historical and educational works while allowing later-identified copyright owners to assert their rights (Rowland 2026).

### Justification and reflection

I support allowing orphan works to be used, but only in a controlled manner. Institutions should document genuine attempts to identify the owner rather than simply claiming they could not be found. If they use the work, I think they assume responsibility for protecting it from unauthorised reproduction or access. If the owner later appears, their rights should be restored immediately where practical, or within a strict timeframe otherwise. This balances public access with the creator's rights rather than treating those rights as permanently lost.

## Artefact 4: Digital Subscriptions and Changing Service Terms 

### Artefact

<img width="1912" height="1030" alt="Screenshot 2026-09-01 111524" src="https://github.com/user-attachments/assets/10e1df50-f53b-4159-835e-005ca6db0e75" />

### Summary

The Week 7 workshop discussed digital media being governed by licence agreements and raised concerns about paid services introducing advertising after customers had subscribed. The class considered whether changing terms after an agreement is fair and how customers should be informed (Galea 2026).

### Justification and reflection

When Amazon Prime was discussed, I immediately thought of YouTube Premium and a Louis Rossmann video I had watched on 24 August about advertising appearing in a paid service (Rossmann 2026). The discussion reinforced a position I had already developed around IoT, subscriptions and legacy equipment. Companies should honour the original agreement and clearly inform customers about proposed changes. If changes are permitted by the agreement, that may be fair, but significant conditions should still be explicit rather than buried within lengthy terms and conditions.
